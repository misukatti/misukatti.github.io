Rendering HDR lightmaps

A Hammerite lightmap holds how much light reaches each texel of a wall, with no ceiling on it. A sunlit courtyard reads around 35 where a back room reads 0.3. Nothing clamps that range on its way to the screen: the last step is Godot's tonemapper, and the default one cuts everything above white off flat. This guide is about getting that step right.

1. How a texel reaches the screen

The bake keeps its light in half floats (FORMAT_RGBAH), so a lamp's hot spot and a corner far from it are both stored as measured. A wall's shader then takes, for each pixel:

  1. the light at that texel - static, the four slots at their lamps' current strength, and ambient;
  2. through the light curve, which decides what each level of light is worth to the eye;
  3. times the surface's own colour;
  4. times hammerite_exposure, what the eye has adapted to (HammeriteShaderGlobals.exposure);
  5. written to EMISSION, in linear light, as large as it came out.

The walls draw with ambient_light_disabled and specular_disabled. What the Environment adds as ambient light or reflections does not reach them: their whole picture is the bake. What the Environment does decide is step 6, the tonemapper, which turns that linear light into the screen's 0 to 1.

2. Use a non-linear tonemapper

With no Environment, or with TONE_MAPPER_LINEAR, anything above 1 is clipped. A patch of sun under a window becomes a white shape with hard edges, a lamp's pool turns white in the middle with no gradient, and a bright coloured light goes to a saturated flat colour before it goes white. The bake measured all of that; the screen throws it away.

A non-linear tonemapper rolls bright light off towards white instead. Use AgX:

var environment := Environment.new()
environment.tonemap_mode = Environment.TONE_MAPPER_AGX
var world_environment := WorldEnvironment.new()
world_environment.environment = environment
add_child(world_environment)

Or add a WorldEnvironment to your level scene in Godot's editor and set Tonemap > Mode to AgX.

  • tonemap_agx_white is the light level that reaches white. Raise it if bright patches still flatten into white shapes - sun through a window is the usual one. Lower it if the whole picture looks dull.
  • tonemap_agx_contrast steepens or flattens the middle of the curve.
  • Filmic and ACES roll off too. AgX takes very bright colours towards white, as film and eyes do, so an orange lamp's hot spot is a pale yellow-white rather than a flat orange.

Hammerite's editor shares the World3D of the scene it is opened in, so the WorldEnvironment in your level scene tonemaps the editor's 3D view as well. Light a level with the tonemapper it will ship with, or every lamp will be tuned for a curve the player never sees.

3. One exposure, not two

hammerite_exposure multiplies the light before the tonemapper, which is what Godot's tonemap_exposure does too. Use one of them and leave the other at 1.

  • HammeriteEyeAdaptation3D drives hammerite_exposure from the light where the camera stands, read from the bake, so a dark room and a courtyard can share one tonemapper.
  • Godot's auto exposure (CameraAttributesPractical.auto_exposure_enabled) measures the rendered screen instead. Do not run both: they will chase each other.

4. The light curve comes first

The light curve shapes the light level itself, before the surface's colour and before exposure. It decides what is shadow: a dip at its dark end makes a corner dark without touching the lit walls. The tonemapper only decides how the brightest light rolls off at the end. Do not reach for tonemap_agx_contrast to make shadows darker - change the curve, which leaves the highlights alone.

5. Glow

Since the walls write their light above 1 where it is bright, Godot's glow works on them as it does on any HDR source: with glow_enabled and glow_hdr_threshold at 1 or a little above, a lamp's hot spot and a sun patch bloom and a dim wall does not. Self-lit materials should multiply by hammerite_exposure (Brush materials and shaders) so they glow in step with the lit world.

6. Checking it

The editor's Lightmap visualisation draws the light alone, with no surface colour and no exposure. A wall that reads well over 1 there is one the tonemapper has to roll off; a level whose lit surfaces sit mostly under 1 will look much the same under Linear and AgX.
