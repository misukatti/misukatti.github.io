Importing .map files

HammeriteMapFileImporter brings a .map - the format TrenchBroom, J.A.C.K. and the Radiants save, classic or Valve 220 - into Hammerite once, to go on editing there. It is made for a project coming from TrenchBroom and func_godot: its textures, its layers and groups and its own entity classes come with it. It is run once, in the Godot editor, from Project → Tools → Import .map File…, which writes the map into the project as a .hmap; the game decides what the map's entities become.

What happens to the world

A .map builds solid in a void; Hammerite carves air out of rock. The importer carves everything the map spans with one air brush, puts each world brush back as solid, and fills every room no entity stands in - the void outside the seal - back in, on a hidden layer of its own. Every brush the author drew is kept as it was drawn.

  • The sealed inside is one room. A .map has none to give it. Give the importer a HammeriteRoomDivider as room_divider and it is divided as it is imported, at every hole through a wall larger than the hole - a doorway, a window - and wherever a door of door_types closes an opening; or the author divides it in the editor with Selection → Divide into Rooms. Each room is air brushes that overlap, and two rooms abut at the hole between them, so it is a portal - see Rooms, portals and line of sight. Quake's e1m1 divides into 39 rooms in 2 s. The editor manual's Dividing a map into rooms is the author's side: checking the result, and dividing and joining by hand.
  • A map that leaks is imported with its void still carved, and says so. func_godot keeps no seal, so a func_godot level often leaks: punch_block's arenas are open to the sky, and its tetrisk levels are walled with clip brushes, which stand in a room rather than close it (below).
  • Brushes of world_classnames (worldspawn, func_group) are the world, and those of detail_classnames (func_detail and its kin) detail. A map with no world brushes at all is carved from its detail: func_godot's own example level is func_detail alone.
  • A brush made only of tool_textures - TrenchBroom's and func_godot's clip, skip, origin, trigger, hint... matched by file name - is left out of the world. An entity made of nothing else, a trigger, keeps them as its volume. caulk and nodraw are not tool textures: a brush of either is structure nobody sees, and seals.
  • A brush of clip, playerclip, monsterclip or weapclip alone is a wall nobody sees, where the game has the map tools' clip type (HammeriteEntitySets.map_tools(), in everything(), and added by HammeriteFgdImporter.register_types()): each comes in as a HammeriteClip of its own, drawn only in the editor, striped in the colour of what it stops, and left out of the bake, which neither seals nor carves. Its Blocks says what it stops - everything, players, monsters or projectiles - and each is a physics layer of the game's own, under Project Settings → Hammerite → Physics → Clip Layers (hammerite/physics/clip_layers/), the first layer for all four until the game says. Without the type a clip brush is left out, as before.
  • TrenchBroom's layers become the map's, hidden and locked as they were, and its groups the map's groups. A layer marked omit from export is left out, as TrenchBroom leaves it out. What is in a group is on the layer its outermost group is on, as TrenchBroom has it. Hammerite's groups hold no groups, so a group within a group is part of the outermost one, the one a click selects in TrenchBroom.

Textures

A face's texture is named by its file, as TrenchBroom wrote its path: base/Floor_Tiles is Floor_Tiles, the name Hammerite's texture library finds Floor_Tiles.png by. The library looks in each of its search paths' own folder and not in the folders within it, so a game that keeps its textures in folders, as TrenchBroom's games do (punch_block's are in textures/dev), names each folder in the paths - a HammeriteLevel's Texture Paths. The import names the textures it found none of. textures renames any the game keeps under another:

importer.textures = {"base/Floor_Tiles": &"floor_tiles_worn", "sky1": &"sky_dusk"}

Each face keeps where its texture lay. TrenchBroom drew a texel a unit; a game whose art is drawn that way sets Project Settings → Hammerite → Textures → Texels Per Unit to 1 (hammerite/textures/texels_per_unit), and a face that was unscaled comes in unscaled, as does one reset or drawn afresh. At Hammerite's default of 4 the map still comes in at its own size, its faces scaled to make up the difference. Art enlarged since is said with texels_per_map_texel. Import with the textures already where your game finds them: a face's offset is worked out in its texture's pixels.

A game's WADs and PAKs

A Quake-engine project keeps its textures in WAD files, and a game in its PAKs, whose maps carry their own. Project → Tools → Unpack Game Textures… in the Godot editor opens any of them - a .wad (WAD2, or Half-Life's WAD3), a .pak, whose WADs and maps are read, or a single map's .bsp - and lists its textures with a picture of each and the file it is written as. Untick any you do not want, choose the folder in the project they go in (res://textures/<file> to begin with) and press Unpack.

  • They are written as PNGs, named as the importer names them, so a map imported afterwards finds them: Quake's {grate is the cut-out !grate.png, *water0 the liquid liquid_water0.png, and Half-Life's !water, its liquid, is liquid_water.png, as the importer names them both. An animation's frames, +0slime to +9slime, are each a texture of their own.
  • A { texture's last palette colour is left unpainted, as the games leave it.
  • Quake draws its palette's last 32 colours at full brightness; a texture with any of them gets a _emission partner holding them alone, so its lamps and panels glow in the bake.
  • Quake's textures are drawn in the one palette its game has: choose gfx/palette.lmp, or the pak0.pak that holds it. A PAK that holds it needs nothing more. Half-Life's carry their own.

HammeriteTextureArchive does the same from a script:

var archive := HammeriteTextureArchive.open("/games/quake/id1/pak0.pak")
archive.extract(archive.get_names(), "res://textures/quake")

Entities

Each classname is looked up in two places, the first answer winning:

  1. entity_types, a classname to a Hammerite type.
  2. A type registered under the classname itself - so a game that registers its FGD's classes by their own names needs nothing.

Brushwork with no type is kept as a group. A point entity with none is left out and counted, by classname, in get_stats()["skipped_classnames"].

Every key the type declares a property of the same name for is copied, as that property's kind of value: a float from "25", a Vector3 turned from the map's axes, a Color from "255 128 0". origin places a point entity, angle turns one that has a rotation, targetname is its name and target its target, as TrenchBroom draws its links. For anything else, entity_converter is called last with the entity and its keys as text, and can leave the entity out by answering false:

func _configure_importer(importer: Object) -> void:
    if importer is not HammeriteMapFileImporter:
        return
    importer.entity_types = {"enemy_grunt": &"guard", "player_spawn": &"player_start"}
    importer.entity_converter = func(entity: HammeriteEntity, keys: Dictionary) -> bool:
        if entity.type == &"guard":
            entity.properties[&"deaf"] = int(keys.get("spawnflags", "0")) & 1 != 0
        return !keys.get("classname", "").begins_with("pickup_")

That is a method of the game's entity set: name the set's script, or the level's entity definitions, as the import dialog's Entity definitions, and every set added is asked to configure the importer before it reads. From a script or a build tool, make your own and call import_file().

Your FGD

A TrenchBroom game describes its entities in an FGD, and a func_godot game keeps that FGD as resources that also say which node each class builds. Hammerite does not read an FGD as it edits: the FGD is ported once into what a Hammerite game writes by hand, an entity set, which is the game's from then on.

In the Godot editor, Project → Tools → Port FGD to Hammerite… reads a .fgd or func_godot's FGD resource and lists its classes. Untick any the game no longer wants, and choose what each is built on:

  • func_godot adapter runs the class's func_godot script or scene as it is (below). The choice for a class that has one.
  • Ported script writes a Hammerite entity script for the class to port its behaviour to. The choice for a TrenchBroom class, which has no code of func_godot's to run.

Port writes the set - res://entities/my_game_entities.gd, class_name MyGameEntities, named after the FGD, unless you change it - and the ported classes' scripts beside it. A script already there is left as it is. Add the set as the HammeriteLevel's Entity Definitions, or from the game's code:

HammeriteEntityTypeRegistry.add_set(MyGameEntities.new())

The set registers each class as an entity type of its own name, which the map importer finds each entity's type by, copying its keys as the types the FGD gave them. Each type's properties are the class's, its base classes' included, with their defaults. The inspector shows them as TrenchBroom did: each by its display name, its description the tooltip, a choice as a list of its labels, flags as a box each, and a target or targetname picked from the map's other entities' names (HammeriteEntityTypeRegistry.key_label, key_doc, key_choices, key_flags and key_picker). The class's size is its box in the editor and its colour its tint. It is a script like any other: change a default, a label or a choice there, or add a type beside them.

To try a game's entities before porting them, HammeriteFgdImporter registers an FGD's classes as they are read. Point the HammeriteLevel's Entity Definitions at the .fgd or resource itself, or:

var fgd := HammeriteFgdImporter.new()
fgd.read_func_godot(load("res://entities/my_game_fgd.tres"))   # or read_fgd_file("res://my_game.fgd")
fgd.register_types()
fgd.configure(importer)   # the solids func_godot merges into the world are the world

configure() also makes func_godot's static geometry the world: a solid class whose node is a StaticBody3D and which has no script, func_geo and its kin. One the importer already takes as detail stays detail.

Your entity scripts, unchanged

A class built on its adapter - HammeriteFuncGodotPointEntity, or for a solid class the brush adapter whose body is the node its class builds - does what func_godot did:

  • It attaches the class's script to the entity's own node (so a door's script moving self moves the door), or builds its scene or engine class as a child, with the script on the scene's root.
  • It hands the script the entity's properties as func_godot hands them: every key the map gave the entity, declared or not, through func_godot_properties and _func_godot_apply_properties(properties), and onto node properties of the same name and type where the class asked for that. A vector is in the map's axes and origin is the map's own text, "176 -640 0". Then it calls _func_godot_build_complete() and puts the node in the class's node groups.
  • All of that happens before the node enters the tree, as a level func_godot built in the editor is saved with it, so a script's _ready sees its properties.
  • A point entity stands, turns and scales as func_godot put its node. A brush entity's node stands where its class's origin type says (the middle of its brushwork by default, or its origin brush), with the brushwork drawn where it is.

Scripts written for func_godot run as they are, so a game can move over first and port later - once its numbers are in Hammerite's units and axes (below).

Two things do not carry over. func_godot names each node entity_<name> beside the others, and a script that finds another entity by that name in its parent finds nothing; the example's info_camera finds its target that way. And TrenchBroom's own keys, _tb_group and the rest, are not handed over, since they are the map's groups and layers now.

Porting a class properly

A class ported to a script is built on that script: its properties are variables, and it has the _set_editor_active and _apply_entity_property every entity has, for the work its func_godot script did to be moved into. To port a class left on its adapter later, write its script and build it on that instead, by changing its classname in the set to the script's class:

var fgd := HammeriteFgdImporter.new()
fgd.read_fgd_file("res://my_game.fgd")
fgd.write_entity_scripts("res://entities", ["func_door"])   # res://entities/func_door.gd, class_name FuncDoor

Porting the FGD again replaces the set, and with it any change made to it since.

Units

func_godot builds a level in Godot's units: it divides brush coordinates and entity origins by the map settings' inverse_scale_factor, 32 by default, so 32 map units are a metre. Hammerite does not. A level keeps the map's units, as Hammerite's own defaults expect - an 18-unit step, a 64-unit grid - so an imported level is inverse_scale_factor times larger than it was under func_godot.

func_godot left entity properties as they were written, so a key's value means what it meant. What has to change is the game's own numbers measured in metres: a player's speed and jump height, a camera's near plane, a script's distances and ranges, gravity, and the size of every model and scene placed in the level. Multiply each by the inverse_scale_factor the game used.

func_godot also lays a map out in other axes: the map's X is Godot's Z under func_godot and Godot's X under Hammerite, so an imported level stands turned a quarter round from where func_godot stood it. Up is up in both. A turn or a move a script makes relative to its own node comes out the same, and so does anything vertical. What a script does in the world's own horizontal directions does not: the map's (x, y, z) is Godot's (y, z, x) under func_godot and (x, z, -y) under Hammerite (HammeriteMapFileImporter.to_hammerite).

Two func_godot projects were brought across to find out what that means in practice:

  • punch_block (inverse_scale_factor 16) builds its levels at runtime and writes its numbers in metres. Its doors rise by their movepos in metres, 0 4 0, which is 4 units in Hammerite and has to be 64, and open when the player is within 5, which has to be 80. Its characters' speed (1), gravity (0.55), view_height (1.75) and step heights (1.1), the player's jump_power (10), its projectiles' radius (0.25), and its player, enemy and button scenes all scale by 16. Its doors only ever move upwards, so the quarter turn changes nothing.
  • func_godot's example project (inverse_scale_factor 32) writes its keys in map units and divides in its scripts (GameManager.INVERSE_SCALE, 1/32), so its door's move_pos and its lights' range are right once that is 1. Its actors' speed (3), jump_strength (5) and GRAVITY (10) and its marsfrog scene scale by 32. Its door turns move_rot on its own node, which comes out the same, but slides by move_pos turned into Godot's axes by its own id_vec_to_godot_vec, which is func_godot's (y, z, x): under Hammerite it has to be (x, z, -y), or the door slides a quarter round from where it should.

Quake's own maps

A map made with Quake's art and Quake's entities - one of id's, or a mod's - is read with Quake's conventions by HammeriteMapFileImporter.quake():

  • a texture's name says what it is: {grate is the cut-out !grate, *water0 the liquid liquid_water0 and a room of water, sky4 the sky sky_sky4, all in lower case (quake_texture_name);
  • Quake's lights, doors, buttons, platforms, triggers and path corners become the entity sets' types, their keys converted - a light's brightness, colour and reach, a door's travel along its angle less its lip.
