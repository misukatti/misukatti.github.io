Hammerite so far: two and a half years in brushes

Hammerite began in March 2024 as a small brush editor. It's now a level editor, a lightmapper, a sound system and a navigation builder for Godot, written in C++, and it has just had two releases in a single week. This is how it got here, including the parts we had to throw away.

2024: a brush editor

The first commit is from 17 March 2024. Within a month there were boxes and cylinders, a clip tool, extrude and a texture browser. It worked the way Valve's Hammer and the Quake editors do: the level starts as empty space, and you add convex solid blocks to it until there's something to stand on.

In November 2024 Hammerite became a Godot addon. The same day, it went into the first commit of City of Thieves, the stealth game we're making with it, as a git submodule. Every feature since has been tried in that game first.

The lightmapper that didn't scale

Lighting came early. In May 2024 Hammerite got a lightmapper: each face cut into patches, light from the lamps, then radiosity, light bouncing from patch to patch, all on the CPU. It looked right on a test room. It did not scale at all. Radiosity has every patch trading light with every other patch it can see, so the work grows much faster than the level, and a real level is a great many patches. Spreading it over threads, that December, didn't change the shape of the problem.

In January 2025 we started again on the GPU, as compute shaders. It took until August to get right: on 26 August the commit says "Working GPU lightmapper", the next day the CPU path was deleted, and the day after that radiosity itself was running on the GPU. The commit message was "Working radiosity!"

Sound, by hand

In November 2025 City of Thieves got its sound. A stealth game lives on it: footsteps through a doorway, a guard who hears you in the next room but not through a metre of stone. We built it the way the Thief games did, with sound travelling room to room through the openings between them. That needs to know what the rooms are and where the doorways are, which an additive editor doesn't.

So every room got an audio zone placed by hand, and every doorway a portal, a quad sized to fit it. It worked, carefully, and every change to a level meant checking the markers again.

August 2026: four addons, and the world inside out

By then the game and the editor had grown into each other: the sound system, for one, was written inside City of Thieves. On 17 August we pulled them apart. Hammerite became separate addons, core, editor and audio, and the sound system moved out of the game into the last of them. Nothing in Hammerite may depend on a particular game, and its test suites check that it doesn't.

A week later came the biggest change in its history. We turned the world inside out: solid became the default, and rooms are carved out of it with air brushes, the way Looking Glass's Dromed built the Thief games. The compiler works out the rooms and the openings between them by itself, so the hand-placed audio zones went, and so did the tool that placed them. That week was 188 commits, and it gets a post of its own.

The fourth addon, navigation, was written the same week, on the carved world from its first commit: walkable ground is simply the floor of the air.

Autumn 2026: three releases

  • 29 September 0.9. The first tagged version, the day after the last of the additive display code was removed.
  • 30 September A native kernel. The heaviest geometry, carving and the walls, moved to optional C++, with GDScript doing the same work where it wasn't installed.
  • 10 October 1.0. The first release, with a supported API and a promise to keep it.
  • 17 October 2.0. Every class native, in one library, seven days after 1.0. The release post has the details.

What we learned

  • Don't make a person say what the geometry already knows. The hand-placed zones worked, but every one was a second copy of the level to keep true.
  • Some algorithms need the right machine. Radiosity on the CPU wasn't a slow version of the right thing; it was the wrong place to do it.
  • A real game is the best test suite. Nearly every decision in Hammerite was made because City of Thieves needed something and the editor couldn't give it yet.
